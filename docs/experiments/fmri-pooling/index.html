Pooling Small-N fMRI: when does merging studies actually work?

Most fMRI studies scan 15–30 people — too few to trust on their own. The obvious fix is to pool many small studies into one shared space. We built the machinery to do that and ran it where the answer should be easy. It came back negative — and the negative turned out to be the finding.

01 · The QuestionCan small studies be pooled into one brain?

The sample-size problem in fMRI is not a secret. Button et al. (2013) showed that the typical N of 15–30 leaves individual studies underpowered and their effects unstable. The proposed remedy, in the naturalistic-stimulus corner of the field, is functional alignment: instead of trusting that the same anatomical voxel does the same job in every brain, learn a transform that puts each person's data into a shared information space, then pool.

That machinery — hyperalignment (Haxby et al., 2020) and the Shared Response Model (Chen et al., 2015) — is proven on narrative films, where different brains watching the same story land on the same high-dimensional trajectory once you rotate them into register. The pitch for pooling is seductive: gather every small open movie-watching dataset, align them, and answer questions no single 20-person study could. Nastase et al. (2020) even showed you can aggregate heterogeneous datasets into one response space.

We wanted to know where that pitch holds and where it breaks. So we did the honest thing: built the alignment pipeline, ran it on a large public movie dataset, and tried to break our own result before believing it.

02 · The MethodThe pipeline, and the test it has to pass

The design is deliberately simple and deterministic, because the claim is about linear algebra, not model capacity. For a set of subjects who all received the same stimulus:

Pipeline

1. Z-score every voxel/vertex's timecourse per subject. 2. Split the stimulus into a train segment (first ⅔) and a held-out test segment (last ⅓). 3. Compute a PCA shared basis (k = 100) from the other subjects' mean train response — never the subject being evaluated (leave-one-subject-out). 4. Fit a per-subject orthogonal Procrustes rotation on train only. 5. Score on the held-out test segment with time-segment matching: can we find the right moment of the stimulus in the group mean, given one subject's response?

The comparison is anatomical-only alignment (everyone in a common anatomical space, no functional rotation) versus functionally aligned (the Procrustes step added). If functional alignment helps, matched accuracy should rise. Statistics: per-subject paired sign test plus a 1000-draw permutation test on the mean difference. Everything runs on public data with fixed seeds; the numbers below are exactly reproducible.

Both datasets are public on OpenNeuro and both use long, continuous naturalistic stimulation. They differ in the way the hypothesis cares about — narrative vs. no narrative — and, unavoidably, in several other ways as well:

A note on rigor: the two datasets live in different reference spaces (surface vs. volume) because that is the form in which each is publicly distributed with all subjects in a common frame. The question — does functional alignment beat anatomical alignment? — is asked within each dataset, against its own anatomical baseline, so the cross-space difference does not confound that comparison. It does, however, mean the between-dataset comparison is not a clean stimulus manipulation — see the limitation in §06.

Both accuracies sit far above the empirical chance level (~0.01), so there is real shared movie signal to find — this is not a floor effect. But the per-subject functional rotation reliably removed accuracy rather than adding it, and the permutation test on the mean difference is p < 0.001. The result was stable across the basis dimension (k = 10, 20, 50, 100), so it is not a tuning artifact.

Why we trust a negative

A negative result is only worth reporting if you have tried to kill it. Before believing this one we fixed a genuine measurement bug (an operator-precedence error that returned the first timepoint's accuracy instead of the mean), then re-ran everything clean. The sign held. More on the controls below.

04 · The ControlsThree ways we tried to prove ourselves wrong

Control 1 · Leakage

Not a leakage artifact

The first pilot computed the PCA basis from a group mean that included the subject being scored — which would trivially cap any alignment benefit. The fix is strict leave-one-subject-out: basis and group mean computed without the evaluated subject. The leakage-free version shows the same negative difference (anatomical 0.061 / aligned 0.035). The negative is not leakage.

Control 2 · Positive control

The pipeline can detect alignment benefit when it exists

We built synthetic data where 40 subjects share a response but each is rotated by a known, recoverable transform. Here functional alignment must win. It does, decisively: anatomical 0.024 / aligned 0.275, Δ = +0.25, 40/40 subjects. The machinery detects alignment benefit when it is present — so the real-data negative is a property of the data, not a broken method.

Control 3 · Dimensionality

Not a ceiling or a bad k

The negative replicates at k = 10, 20, 50 on a 40-subject subset (Δ between −0.006 and −0.017). The effect does not flip at any tested dimensionality, so it is not a shared-space bottleneck.

Interim read

On a non-narrative film, anatomical alignment already captures the shared response and the functional rotation only injects noise. That matches a specific prediction: hyperalignment earns its keep on the idiosyncratic fine-grained topography that narrative drives, and Koyaanisqatsi has little of it. But that is an interpretation, not a result. The result needs a narrative stimulus.

05 · The Narrative TestForrest Gump, heard not watched: does the story flip the sign?

This is the discriminating test — with one wrinkle that has to be named up front: the StudyForrest data available in a common group template is the 7T audio description, so the narrative arrives through the ears, not the eyes. The literature predicts functional alignment should help on a narrative stimulus. If it does, the Koyaanisqatsi negative is a boundary condition on the stimulus, not a flaw in the pooling idea — and the two results together map where pooling works.

On the single fully-downloaded audio-description run (run-01, 8 subjects, 151 held-out TRs), neither alignment condition recovered much signal — both sit just above the nominal floor (~0.007) and far below the Koyaanisqatsi accuracies. Functional alignment gives a statistically meaningless Δ = +0.002 (5/8 subjects, permutation p = 0.86).

Read: this run is too small to decide the hypothesis. It does not show the predicted narrative sign-flip, but at N = 8 with a near-floor baseline it cannot rule one out either. The discriminating test needs the full 8-run dataset. Computed from public data with the same seeds and code path as the Koyaanisqatsi result — no refitting, no silent parameter changes.

06 · What It MeansThe boundary, stated precisely

One result is solid, and it is the one most pooling proposals skip past: functional alignment is not free signal. On a stimulus whose shared response is already anatomically aligned, the extra rotation reliably costs accuracy (Koyaanisqatsi, N = 93, Δ = −0.026, p < 0.001), and the pipeline provably can detect a benefit when one exists (synthetic control, Δ = +0.25). Any plan to merge small studies has to ask first whether the shared signal lives in anatomy or in idiosyncratic topography — because the answer decides whether alignment helps or hurts.

The narrative test, run honestly, came back underpowered rather than decisive. On the one fully-downloaded Forrest Gump audio-description run (8 subjects), both conditions sat near the floor and alignment gave a null Δ = +0.002 (5/8, p = 0.86). We therefore cannot yet say whether the Koyaanisqatsi negative is a stimulus boundary (the interesting case — alignment helps only where narrative drives idiosyncratic topography) or a more general property. What we can rule out is a large narrative benefit on this slice: the effect, if real, is smaller than the synthetic-control ceiling and needs the full 8-run dataset to detect. That is a measurement-plan statement, not a retreat — the next run is already scoped, and this page will be updated when it lands.

Limitation · narrative is not isolated by this design

The stimulus-boundary reading — alignment helps where narrative drives idiosyncratic topography — is the interpretation we find most plausible, but this pair of datasets cannot separate it from the alternatives. Going from Koyaanisqatsi to StudyForrest changes narrative and at least four other things at once: modality (visual film vs. auditory audio description), field strength (3T vs. 7T), reference space (fsaverage5 surface vs. volumetric group template), subject count (93 vs. 8), and how much of the stimulus was pooled (full moviewatching run vs. one run of eight).

So narrative is one of at least five candidate explanations for any difference between the two datasets, and this design cannot tell them apart. That does not make the boundary reading wrong — it makes it untested. Isolating it needs a comparison that holds modality, field strength and space fixed and varies only the stimulus: for example, narrative vs. scrambled-narrative within a single acquisition.

What would change our mind

A clean sign-flip on the full StudyForrest dataset (aligned > anatomical, sign test and permutation p < 0.05) would confirm the stimulus boundary and map where pooling earns its keep. A replicated null at full power would push the conclusion toward "alignment rarely helps on this matching task" — and would make the synthetic positive control, not real data, the outlier worth explaining. Either outcome is publishable; the point is to report whichever the data support.

07 · ReproduceWhere the data came from and what we did to it

The data are public and the analysis is deterministic, so the numbers above are recoverable from scratch. The scripts and result JSONs themselves are not yet published in this repository — they live on the research machine that ran the analysis (see the provenance note below). What follows is enough to rebuild the pipeline independently: the exact accessions, the derivative type, how the subset was chosen, and what was computed.

Provenance · where the data came from

AOMIC-ID1000 — the Amsterdam Open MRI Collection, OpenNeuro accession ds003097, snapshot v1.2.0 (Snoek et al., 2021). Stimulus: a non-narrative visual excerpt of Koyaanisqatsi (no story, no dialogue), acquired at 3T (task-moviewatching). We used the distributed fMRIPrep derivatives resampled to the fsaverage5 cortical surface, both hemispheres (L+R).

StudyForrest — OpenNeuro accession ds000113, snapshot 1.3.0 (Hanke et al., 2014). Stimulus: a spoken German audio description of Forrest Gump, acquired at 7T — auditory only, no visual presentation. We used the grpbold7Tad group-template derivative (non-linearly aligned BOLD in a common volumetric group space), masked to the group brain-mask intersection (493,857 voxels), run-01, 451 TRs, 8 subjects.

Both were pulled directly from the OpenNeuro file API. There is no bespoke preprocessing: the distributed derivatives are used as shipped, because the claim is about alignment on top of a common space, not about preprocessing choices.

Subset selection · why 93 and why 8

AOMIC — 93 of ~927. AOMIC-ID1000 ships moviewatching derivatives for roughly 927 subjects. The 93 used here are a bandwidth-limited download subsample — ~2.4 GB of fsaverage5 L+R derivatives — not a property of the dataset. An earlier version of this page described "93 subjects with complete fsaverage5 moviewatching derivatives" as though 93 were all the dataset offered; that was wrong. The 93 are the subjects whose L+R pair had downloaded completely when the analysis ran, and nothing was excluded on the basis of its result — but the subsample was not drawn under a pre-specified randomisation, so it should be treated as convenience, not a random sample. A re-run at full N is open to anyone with the bandwidth and would be a stronger test than ours.

StudyForrest — 8 subjects, run-01 only. The group-template audio-description data spans 8 runs; at the time of the analysis only run-01 had downloaded completely for every subject (several later runs remained truncated .part files, and a container restart OOM-killed the first pass). The loader skips truncated .nii.gz files rather than silently reading partial volumes. This is the direct cause of the underpowered narrative test described in §05.

Analysis · what was done to the data

Within each dataset, per-vertex/per-voxel timecourses are z-scored, split into a contiguous train (first ⅔) and test (last ⅓) segment, and reduced to a k=100 PCA shared basis computed from the other subjects' mean train response. Each subject gets an orthogonal Procrustes rotation into that basis — the hyperalignment step — fit on train only, under strict leave-one-subject-out.

The pooling comparison is then the held-out time-segment-matching accuracy of anatomical-only alignment versus functionally aligned data, scored per subject, tested with a paired sign test and a 1000-draw permutation test on the mean difference. The synthetic positive control applies the same code path to 40 simulated subjects sharing a response under known random rotations.

Pipeline parameters 
shared basis      PCA, k = 100
alignment       orthogonal Procrustes (per subject, fit on train only)
design          leave-one-subject-out (basis + group mean exclude the scored subject)
split           train = first 2/3 of TRs, test = last 1/3 (contiguous)
evaluation      time-segment matching on the held-out test segment
statistics      paired sign test + 1000-draw permutation on the mean difference
seed            42
chance          empirical (autocorrelation-aware) ≈ 0.01; nominal 1/nTR

Honesty log

Four errors were caught and fixed during this work and are recorded rather than smoothed over. (1) A measurement bug made the first pilot's accuracies unreliable; it was found on adversarial re-read and everything was re-run. (2) The dataset paper for AOMIC-ID1000 was initially cited under a wrong DOI that resolves to an unrelated paper; it was corrected to Snoek et al. (2021) after a title match. The references below are the verified set. (3) The first published version of this page described the StudyForrest grpbold7Tad data as a film the subjects watched. It is the 7T audio description — auditory only, no visual stimulus. The page's own citation (Hanke et al., 2014, "an audio movie") said so; we misread it. Corrected throughout. (4) The same version presented "93 subjects" as the dataset's complement of complete moviewatching derivatives; AOMIC ships roughly 927, and 93 was our download subsample. Corrected in §07.
